# Possible NYT Headlines

---

**"Morality Is Perception, Scientists Find — and Your Ethics Are Just How Noisy Your Brain Is"**
A new study shows moral judgment obeys the same psychophysical laws as seeing or hearing: the brain treats the choice between lives as a perceptual comparison, not a philosophical calculation. What we call a "utilitarian" person simply has less internal noise; a "deontological" one has more. Good and evil, it turns out, are measured the way we measure everything else.

---

**"Morality Has a Formula, and It Looks Like a Vision Test"**
The same mathematical law that describes how we perceive differences in weight or light turns out to govern moral judgment. People don't count lives — they perceive ratios, under noise. A new psychophysical model unifies decades of conflicting findings about moral psychology in a single equation.

---

**"The Trolley Problem Is a Perception Problem"**
Whether you'd pull a lever to save five people by killing one isn't a window into your soul — it's a measurement of your internal noise. New research reframes one of philosophy's most famous thought experiments as a psychophysical discrimination task, no different in principle from telling apart two shades of gray.

---

**"We're All Trying to Do the Greater Good. We're Just Noisy About It."**
A new model proposes that humans universally aim for the outcome that saves the most lives — but perceptual uncertainty about the value of human life gets in the way. Deontological responses aren't a different moral strategy; they're what happens when the brain can't tell which option is better.

---

**"Why Losing Feels Worse Than Winning: It's Geometry, Not Psychology"**
Loss aversion — the finding that losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains — has never had a mechanistic explanation. A new study shows it falls out automatically from the same perceptual model that explains moral judgment, requiring no special parameters or emotional circuitry.

---

**"A.I. Can Pass a Moral Reasoning Test. It Still Doesn't Think Like Us."**
Frontier language models reproduce some patterns of human moral judgment, but stress-testing reveals they do it by tallying harm and utility — not by perceiving ratios the way humans do. The finding suggests that whatever human moral cognition is, it cannot be approximated by scaling up data alone.
