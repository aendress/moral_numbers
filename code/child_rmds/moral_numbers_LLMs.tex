\subsection{Methods}\label{methods}

We compared the responses of humans to those of several frontier (i.e., state-of-the-art) large language models (LLMs). We focused on three experiments: In Experiment 2, we asked whether LLMs are sensitive to the \emph{beneficiary:victim} Weber ratio in moral and economic scenarios. In Experiment 3, we directly pitted Weber ratios against harm (the number of victims in Experiment 3a) and utility (the net number of survivors in Experiment 3b). Finally, in Experiment 6, we tested how LLMs react to manipulations of the vividness of the victims' deaths.

\subsubsection{Models and parameters}\label{models-and-parameters}

In Experiments 2 and 6, we tested GPT-4o mini, GPT-4.1 mini, GPT-5 mini (minimal reasoning), GPT-5 mini (medium reasoning), GPT-5 mini (high reasoning), Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 2.5 flash, Mistral Medium, Deepseek, and Qwen-plus. We chose these models because they reflect a range of jurisdictions (China, France and USA) and thus potentially differences in training corpora and alignment procedures (i.e., post-training procedures used to shape model behavior according to values determined by the model creators and their jurisdictions). We used different versions of OpenAI's GPT models because, during initial non-systematic exploration, some models seemed more deontological than others, and because this allowed us to set the reasoning effort parameter in GPT-5 mini. (This parameter controls how many internal and intermediate reasoning steps the model generates before producing its final response.) We also explored several smaller models run on a local computer through ollama (\url{https://ollama.com/}), namely Llama 3.2 (3.2B), Mistral (7.2B), and Mistral nemo (12.2B).

Experiment 3 was simulated separately. In our initial simulations of Experiment 3, the system prompt describing the response scale contained an error (both ends of the scale favored Option 2), and we thus reran these simulations with a corrected prompt (see Table \ref{tab:LLM-system-prompt-print}). As some of the models used in Experiments 2 and 6 had been retired by then, we used the current mid-tier models of the same providers, namely Claude Sonnet 5.5 (reasoning effort low, medium, and high), Deepseek flash (reasoning effort off, medium, and high), GPT-6 luna (reasoning effort low, medium, and high), Gemini 3.8 flash (reasoning effort low, medium, and high), Mistral Medium 3.5, and Qwen3.7-plus (reasoning effort off and on), as well as the same local models. In these simulations, we also corrected the wording error in the economic version of the \emph{Aqueduct in the desert} scenario (see SI \ref{app:scenario-errors-exp3}), which human participants had seen in its erroneous form. Further, inspection of preliminary simulations suggested that some models might have confused the response scale (1--6) with the option labels (1 vs.~2). To avoid such confusions, we switched the option labels to A vs.~B. Finally, as in the human experiments, we counterbalanced the order of the two options across simulated participants, such that half of them saw the high-ratio option as Option A and half of them as Option B (in our initial simulations, it was always presented first).

For models that support these options, we used the parameters given in Table \ref{tab:LLM-option-print} or their equivalents. The system prompts (general instructions on how the model should respond, passed to the model prior to the experimental trials) are given in Table \ref{tab:LLM-system-prompt-print}.

\subsubsection{Procedure}\label{procedure}

We kept the procedure as closely as possible to the human experiments, where participants rated multiple scenarios in each experiment. We achieved this by treating trials as an ongoing, incremental conversation. On the first trial, the API calls contained the system prompt as well as the first scenario. On the second trial, they contained the system prompt, the first scenario together with the model's answer on the first trial (provided as a message from the \emph{assistant} role or the equivalent in other models), as well as the second scenario. Finally, on the last trial, the API calls contained the system prompt, all previous scenarios together with the model's answers, as well as the last scenario. The main procedural difference to the human experiments is thus that all contrasts (e.g., moral vs.~economic in Experiments 2 and 3; vivid vs.~neutral in Experiment 6) are treated as between-participant contrasts rather than within-participant contrasts as the latter would not be meaningful in models.

As in the human experiments, our simulations used a latin square design, spanned by the available scenarios in each cell and the number conditions (see SI \ref{app:design}). In Experiment 6, and as in the human experiments, we used two counterbalanced sets of number conditions, involving different numbers of victims and beneficiaries.

For each design cell in Experiments 2 and 6, we obtained 10 simulated participants (i.e., independent conversations with the models) for each model. In Experiment 3, the Latin square was additionally crossed with the order of the two options (high-ratio option presented first vs.~second), and we obtained 5 sets of independent responses per cell. Overall, this resulted in the numbers of cells, (simulated) participants, trials per participant, and responses given in Table \ref{tab:number-of-trials-print}, with a total of 1,160 simulated participants and 13,520 responses.

To validate the LLM results, we recorded the number of valid responses for each experiment and condition, where a valid response is a single digit between 1 and 6 with no additional words, punctuation, or explanations (defined by the system prompts shown in Table \ref{tab:LLM-system-prompt-print}). In Experiment 3, Claude Sonnet 5.5 (and, in a single trial, Gemini 3.8 Flash) often answered with a justification followed by a rating (e.g., ``\ldots{} Gut says option B. 4''); in these cases, we used the final rating if it followed the end of a sentence. As shown in Table \ref{tab:verify-llm-results}, when excluding Claude Sonnet 5.5, data loss was minimal (at most 0.8\%). In Experiment 3b, Claude Sonnet 5.5 returned many undefined responses or responses with extraneous justifications beyond the requested numerical values, leading to loss of between 1.5 and 12.4\% of the data. Further, when these text answers were fed back into the ongoing conversations, some later requests were blocked by a filter of the Anthropic API, which flagged them as attempts to reverse engineer or duplicate model outputs. As this pattern replicated across reasoning efforts, it appears that Claude Sonnet 5.5 has difficulties following instructions in Experiment 3b, but showed similarly low rates of data loss as did the other models in the other experiments, including Experiment 3a.

\subsection{Results}\label{results}

In the simulations below, we asked whether the models reproduced the human pattern \emph{qualitatively}, as we had no particular expectation that LLMs might provide a quantitative fit to human behavior. We were thus mainly concerned with the question of whether the models showed preferences in the same direction as humans, and whether these preferences were affected by the same factors.

\paragraph{Frontier models}\label{frontier-models}

In Experiment 2, we asked whether the models would be sensitive to the \emph{beneficiary:victim} Weber ratio in moral and economic scenarios. The results for frontier models and the binarized ratings (responses transformed into acceptable/unacceptable decisions on which our model is based) are shown in Figure \ref{fig:plot-llm-results-exp4-ratio-cloud}a. For moral scenarios, most LLMs showed some sensitivity to Weber ratios, though the quality of fit to our psychophysical model varied. However, some models were much more likely to endorse a sacrificial decision than humans, namely GPT-4.1 mini, GPT-5 mini (with all reasoning effort settings) and Qwen-plus.

In contrast, for economic scenarios, Gemini-2.5 flash and GPT-5 mini (all reasoning effort settings) showed no sensitivity to Weber ratios when considering binarized responses. Nevertheless, Figure \ref{fig:plot-llm-results-exp4-ratio-cloud}b shows that, when considering the raw (rather than binarized) responses, these models also showed some limited sensitivity to Weber ratios.

In contrast to the frontier models, the (smaller) local models showed no discernible sensitivity to Weber ratios, neither in binary ratings (see Figure \ref{fig:plot-llm-results-exp4-ratio-local}a) nor in raw ratings (see Figure \ref{fig:plot-llm-results-exp4-ratio-local}b). However, most did show higher acceptability rates for economic than for moral decisions.

Accordingly, and as shown in Table \ref{tab:print-fits-exp2}, when fitting the model behavior to our decision model, the noise parameter (\(w\)) was higher in the moral than in the economic condition for most models, with the exception of Llama 3.2 (3.2B), where the two values were comparable.

\paragraph{Summary}\label{summary}

Taken together, these results suggest that LLMs can show some sensitivity to Weber ratios in both moral and economic decisions, at least for large frontier models, but not for smaller local models. Further, most models rated economic sacrifices as more acceptable than moral sacrifices. However, the fit to our psychophysical model was of variable quality.

\subsubsection{Experiment 3}\label{experiment-3}

In Experiment 3, participants had to choose between two options. In Experiment 3a, one option had a higher \emph{beneficiary:victim} Weber ratio and a higher number of victims (i.e., harm), while the other had a lower \emph{beneficiary:victim} Weber ratio and also lower harm. In Experiment 3b, we similarly pitted Weber ratios against utility. In moral situations, humans prefer to maximize Weber ratios over minimizing harm or maximizing utility. In economic situations, in contrast, they still prioritize Weber ratios over harm, but prioritize utility over Weber ratios.

The results from the LLM simulations are shown in Figures \ref{fig:plot-llm-results-exp2-forest} and \ref{fig:plot-llm-exp3-moral-economic} (see Table \ref{tab:print-exp2-descriptives-print-details} for detailed results). In Experiment 3a (where we pitted Weber ratios against harm), the frontier models reproduced the human pattern. Like humans, they preferred the option with the higher \emph{beneficiary:victim} ratio over the option with the lower harm in both moral and economic situations, and they did so irrespective of the order of the options (with the exception of Mistral Medium 3.5, which preferred the high-ratio option only when it was presented as the second option; Table \ref{tab:print-exp2-descriptives-print-details}). However, while humans preferred the high-ratio option even more in economic than in moral situations, many models did not reproduce this increase (DeepSeek Flash and GPT-6 luna at all reasoning levels, Qwen3.7-plus with reasoning) or even showed a slightly increased ratio preference in the moral condition (Gemini 3.8 Flash at all reasoning levels, Qwen3.7-plus without reasoning; Table \ref{tab:llm-exp3-order-effects-print}).

In Experiment 3b (where we pitted Weber ratios against utility), humans preferred the high-ratio option in moral situations, but the high-utility option in economic situations. This difference between moral and economic situations was found in both option orders (Table \ref{tab:llm-exp3-condition-effects-by-order-print}). Only one model, Qwen3.7-plus with reasoning, reproduced this pattern. It preferred utility in economic situations and the high-ratio option in moral situations. The difference between moral and economic situations was reliable in both option orders. (When the high-ratio option was presented first, its preference for utility in economic situations was only marginally significant, \emph{p} = .051.) Without reasoning, Qwen3.7-plus preferred utility in both moral and economic situations.

None of the other models reproduced the human pattern, although some models showed a greater ratio preference in the moral than in the economic condition (Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6 luna at all reasoning levels; Table \ref{tab:llm-exp3-condition-effects-by-order-print}), even though this tendency was only marginal or not significant in some option orders. To the extent that the frontier models showed preferences that were consistent across the option orders, they preferred utility over \emph{beneficiary:victim} ratios in both moral and economic situations (e.g., GPT-6 luna and Gemini 3.8 Flash at all reasoning levels, Qwen3.7-plus without reasoning, though the latter results were marginal when the high-ratio option was presented second); no frontier model consistently preferred the high-ratio option in both option orders (Table \ref{tab:print-exp2-descriptives-print-details} and Figure \ref{fig:plot-llm-exp3-moral-economic-by-order}). The preferences of the other frontier models depended strongly on the option order.

Although Claude Sonnet 5.5 was asked to respond with a single number, it often volunteered a justification for its choice in Experiment 3b. We took advantage of these unrequested justifications to examine what Claude spontaneously reasoned about. Claude almost always referred to the net gain of the two options (in 87.4\% to 99.6\% of these responses), and mentioned ratios in 43\% to 54\% of them. In contrast, the word \emph{harm} appeared in only 1.9\% to 4.9\% of these responses (although Claude often referred to the number of lives or resources lost). However, even though Claude systematically computed utility, it tended to decide against maximizing utility and, on average, favored the high-ratio option in both conditions of Experiment 3b, although this preference depended considerably on the order of the options. It should be noted that these justifications are not a random sample of Claude's decisions, as they mostly occurred early in the conversations and when Claude appeared to be torn between the options.

These results are somewhat surprising, as, to our knowledge, no normative theory of moral or economic decision making prescribes decisions based on ratios. Nevertheless, Claude frequently treated ratios as a relevant consideration, suggesting that it acquired, either from its training data or from post-training alignment, the notion that ratios are relevant for decision making. This might reflect ratio-based reasoning in some of the real-world data it was trained on, although we cannot rule out that it reflects research on ratio effects in decision making (including, possibly, earlier versions of the present work).

\paragraph{Summary}\label{summary-1}

In Experiment 3, most models showed qualitatively different behaviors from humans. Though the frontier models reproduced the human preference for \emph{beneficiary:victim} ratios over harm in Experiment 3a, one model (Mistral Medium 3.5) showed this preference only in one option order, and many models did not reproduce the greater preference for ratios in the economic condition.

In Experiment 3b, with the exception of Qwen3.7-plus with reasoning, no model reproduced the human pattern, where humans preferred \emph{beneficiary:victim} ratios over utility in moral situations, but utility over ratios in economic situations. To the extent that the frontier models showed preferences that were consistent across the option orders, they preferred utility in both moral and economic situations. Further, even Qwen3.7-plus with reasoning failed to reproduce the increased preference for ratios in the economic condition of Experiment 3a.

In both experiments, the local models mainly showed effects of the order of the options.

\subsubsection{Experiment 6}\label{experiment-6}

Experiment 6 assessed how acceptability ratings depend on \emph{beneficiary:victim} ratios when the deaths of the victims and the beneficiaries are equally salient vs.~when the deaths of the victims are made more vivid and salient. Our critical measure is the risk ratio comparing acceptability rates when the deaths of the victims are vividly portrayed versus when they are presented with the same neutral language as the beneficiaries, \(\mathrm{RR}= \frac{P_{\text{acceptable, vivid}}}{P_{\text{acceptable, neutral}}}\). In humans, this risk ratio is positively correlated with the \emph{beneficiary:victim} Weber ratio, suggesting that the effect of victim salience decreases for higher Weber ratios.

\paragraph{Summary}\label{summary-2}

Overall, these results suggest that the models generally reproduced the correlation between the risk ratio and the Weber ratio, though the quantitative fit is of variable quality.

\subsection{Overall Summary}\label{overall-summary}

The frontier LLMs showed several key similarities to human moral judgment. Most models demonstrated sensitivity to \emph{beneficiary:victim} Weber ratios, greater acceptance of sacrifices in economic versus moral decisions, and a decreasing effect of victim salience at higher Weber ratios. However, the quantitative fit to the psychophysical model varied considerably across models, and some models failed to show expected patterns.

These similarities appeared to depend on model size and presumably on massive amounts of training data (or on differences in alignment processes), as the smaller local models largely failed to reproduce human behavior. Local models showed no discernible sensitivity to Weber ratios in Experiment 2 and exhibited inconsistent patterns across other experiments.

However, with the exception of Qwen3.7-plus with reasoning, all models behaved qualitatively differently from humans in Experiment 3b. To the extent that the other models showed a consistent preference, they preferred utility over ratios in both moral and economic conditions. Further, even Qwen3.7-plus with reasoning reproduced the results of Experiment 3a only partially.
